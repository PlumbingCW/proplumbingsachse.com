Repiping replaces the water supply pipes in your home. It is a bigger job than a repair, so it should be a considered one. The signs are usually clear: repeated leaks, discolored water, low pressure and pipes you know are made from a material with a known failure history.

Repiping in Sachse, TX covers whole-house projects and partial runs, and the plan depends on the home, its foundation and the pipe in the walls. A plumber looks at the system first and tells you whether a repair, a partial repipe or a full one makes sense.

Signs your pipes need replacing

Repeated pinhole leaks

A second or third leak in the same system suggests corrosion is widespread.

Rusty or brown water

Corroded galvanized steel releases rust into the water.

Low pressure at every fixture

Mineral and rust buildup narrow the pipe inside.

Known problem material

Polybutylene and some old galvanized systems have a history of failure.

Neither is right for every home. A plumber checks your water chemistry, your wall access and your budget before recommending one.

Repiping a slab home

In homes on concrete slabs, the old supply lines may run under the floor. A repipe usually abandons those lines and runs new ones through the attic and walls. That avoids breaking concrete and is the usual reason homeowners with repeated slab leaks choose it. Our slab leak repair page explains when one leak is a repair and when it is a sign of a system problem.

What a repipe involves

1

Inspect and plan

The plumber checks existing pipe, access points and fixtures, then maps a new route.

2

Open access

Small openings in drywall allow pipe to be run, and are planned to limit patching.

3

Install new lines

New supply lines are run from the main to each fixture and a manifold or branch layout is set.

4

Test and restore

The system is pressure tested, water is restored and the plumber shows you the new valves.

Partial repipe or full repipe

A partial repipe replaces the worst section, such as one bathroom group or the lines under a slab. It works when the rest of the system is in good shape. A full repipe is the answer when pipe throughout the house is failing, because patching one area leaves the next one to leak. The plumber should tell you what the evidence supports.

Safety steps during a repipe

  • Know where the new main valve will be and test it after the work.
  • Keep pets and children away from open walls and tools.
  • Ask whether any old pipe or insulation in the walls could contain asbestos, and have it tested before disturbance.
  • Open every faucet briefly after water is restored to clear air and debris.
Ask the City of Sachse building department which permits and inspections apply, and verify the plumber with the Texas State Board of Plumbing Examiners. The city enforces a model plumbing code such as the UPC or the IPC, and it decides what the inspector will check.

Common questions

How long does a repipe take?

Many homes take a few days, depending on size and access. The plumber should give you a schedule and tell you when your water will be off.

Do I have to move out during a repipe?

Most people stay. Water is off for part of each day, and the plumber plans around it.

Will repiping damage my walls?

It requires some openings, which are planned to be small and are patched afterward. Painting is a separate step.

How do I know what pipe I have?

Look at visible pipe near the meter, water heater or under a sink. Copper is reddish brown, galvanized is gray and PEX is red, blue or white plastic.

Is PEX safe for drinking water?

Yes, when it is listed for potable water and installed to code. The plumber uses approved products.
